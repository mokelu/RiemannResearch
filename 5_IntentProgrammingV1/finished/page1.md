# Chapter 1. The Problem

Programming today starts with the machine. 

A programmer has to tell the computer exactly what to do.

They define data.
They write functions.
They create objects.
They connect events.
They manage state.
They describe how everything should work.

The computer then follows those instructions.

This works very well. But it creates a gap between what a person wants and what the computer needs to be told.

A person might say:

> “When the button is pressed, tell me.”

That is a very simple idea.

But in a normal programming language, the programmer has to turn that idea into many smaller instructions.

They have to find the button.

They have to listen for the press.

They have to decide what happens after the press.

They have to connect the button to some function.

They have to send the result somewhere.

The person never thought about any of those things.

They only described what they wanted.

The problem is therefore simple:

> **People think about things and relationships. Computers are usually programmed through instructions and procedures.**

Intent programming starts from the other side.

Instead of asking:

> “How do I tell the computer how to do this?”

we ask:

> **“What if the things in our software world could participate in the computation themselves?”**
